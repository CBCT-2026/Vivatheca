Class I, II and III

Dentists sort bites into three groups, called Angle's classes. They look at where the big upper back tooth (first molar) meets the big lower back tooth.1 If the jaw bones themselves are out of line, dentists call it a skeletal Class II or Class III.

Class I (normal)In balanceUpper front teeth just in front of the lower ones
Class IILower jaw back, upper jaw forward, or bothLarge gap: upper front teeth well in front
Class IIILower jaw forward, upper jaw back, or bothLower front teeth level with or in front of the upper ones

Braces can move teeth, but not the jaw bones. If your jaws are too far out of line, you may need jaw surgery as well as braces.89

What the surgeon does

Lower jaw split (BSSO, short for bilateral sagittal split osteotomy). The surgeon works inside your mouth. They split each side of the lower jaw along its length. The outer layer of bone comes apart from the inner layer. The inner layer holds the teeth and the nerve to your lip and chin. The front part, with the teeth, can then slide forward (Class II) or back (Class III).345

Upper jaw cut (called a Le Fort I osteotomy). The surgeon cuts the upper jaw in a line above the tooth roots. The cut runs from the side of the nose to behind the last back tooth. The part with the upper teeth can then move forward, back, up or down.6

Chin surgery (called a genioplasty). The surgeon cuts across the chin bone. The cut is below the tooth roots and the nerves to the lower lip. Then they slide the tip of the chin forward, back, up or down.1314 It is an extra step. Not every jaw surgery needs it. Surgeons look at the chin on its own, apart from the bite. When they move the lower jaw forward, they often move the chin forward too, for a better side view of the face.15 When the chin still looks too big, they can move it back or make it shorter.13 Some people with a Class II bite have a chin that already sticks out. There is no agreed best way to treat this.16

Small titanium plates and screws hold the bone in place while it heals.9 The plates are thin strips of round screw holes joined by narrow bridges. The surgeon bends each plate to fit the bone. The short screws go only into the outer layer of bone.17 In most cases the result lasts. But the jaw can slip back a little after surgery. How much depends on which way the surgeon moved the jaw.7 See how plates and screws go in, step by step.

What treatment involves

  • In most cases, surgeons wait until your jaws have stopped growing.89
  • Most people wear braces before and after surgery. Orthodontics Australia says braces most often stay on for about 6 to 9 months after surgery. The whole treatment takes about 2 years.9
  • A leaflet from Cambridge University Hospitals (NHS) gives these numbers. About 10 in 100 people have tingling or numbness in the lip or chin for several weeks. Fewer than 1 in 100 have changed feeling for more than a year, and it may not go away.11

Your orthodontist and jaw surgeon will explain your own plan, risks and healing time.

For clinicians: how the models were made

  • Skeletal class: one common cephalometric measure is the ANB angle; Steiner gave about 2 degrees as the mean for a balanced face.2
  • In the UK, need for NHS-funded treatment is often scored with the Index of Orthognathic Functional Treatment Need (IOFTN).1012
  • Base: one adult skull, mandible and permanent teeth (no third molars) from Z-Anatomy. Normal = this anatomy with one change to the chin, sized from published norms. On the Z-Anatomy mandible, lower incisor edge to menton (Ii-Me) is 33.3 mm; in 60 Class I adults with balanced faces the mean was 44.78 mm (men 46.97, women 42.58).18 In all seven models the bone below the lowest anterior root tip is stretched down 12.5 mm at the midline so that Ii-Me = 44.78 mm. The stretch fades out to the side so that the mental foramen ends 14.98 mm above the lower border (was 11.1 mm), the CBCT mean for Class I adults with a normal face height.19 Teeth are unchanged. One mean is used for everyone, so differences by sex and face type are not shown.
  • Class II: the tooth-bearing mandible is moved 6 mm back with a smooth warp that fades over the anterior ramus, so condyles and upper ramus stay in place. No vertical or chin change.
  • Class III: maxilla 3 mm back (fading out above the Le Fort I level and towards the pterygoid plates) and mandibular body 5 mm forward. Mouth opened 2.75 degrees about the condyles so the teeth do not intersect.
  • Surgery: Class II = BSSO advancement 6 mm. Class III = Le Fort I advancement 3 mm + BSSO setback 5 mm, with overlapping proximal bone trimmed. Movements reverse the deformity exactly, so the after models are back in the normal occlusion.
  • Cuts are planes: Le Fort I about 4 mm above the canine root tip; BSSO medial cut 5 mm above the occlusal plane, sagittal split lateral to the roots, buccal cut between first and second molar, lingual split running down to the lower border in front of the angle (short lingual split, as described by Hunsuck).5 Real cuts are curved and differ by surgeon.
  • Movement sizes are example values chosen for teaching, not taken from one study. Not shown: soft tissue, the inferior alveolar and mental nerves, condylar seating, rotation of the occlusal plane.
  • Genioplasty (optional after models): one horizontal sliding osteotomy, sloping down backwards from the front of the chin to exit the lower border at the premolars; segment about 1.9 cm3. Class II: advanced 5 mm; Class III: set back 4 mm, both along the cut plane, so the advancement also shortens the chin slightly (0.8 mm) and the setback lengthens it (0.7 mm).1314 Position: the cut runs about 16 mm below the mental foramen. 5–6 mm below the foramen is the distance most often recommended, but the nerve canal loops below the foramen (on average 4.5 mm in a cadaver study)20, and a CBCT study of 634 half-mandibles found that 7.06, 8.01 and 9.12 mm below the foramen left a 2.5%, 0.5% and 0.0005% risk of reaching the canal.21 The model cut is below all of these. We found no study that gives a set distance below the root tips; the model keeps 6.4 mm below the canine and 6.7 mm below the incisor root tips. In these models the jaw movements already return the bone to the normal skull, so the genioplasty shows the operation itself, not a need in this skull. Real osteotomies are often curved or stepped and extend further back.
  • Plates (Vivatheca fixation standard, /fixation/): 2.0 mm system miniplates, 1.0 mm thick (the thinnest of the 1.0–2.0 mm Mini Plate 2.0 range)23, a countersunk ring round each hole joined by bridges, with dome-headed cross-recess 2.0 mm screws, monocortical, 5 mm long or shorter where the model bone is thinner.1722 Placed on Champy's lines, below the tooth apices and away from the inferior alveolar canal.22 Le Fort I: four L-plates, at the piriform rim and the zygomaticomaxillary buttress on each side, the usual layout (two plates at the piriform rim were equally stable in a cohort of 39).24 BSSO: one 4-hole extended miniplate on each side across the buccal step25; bicortical screws are an equally stable alternative after setback.26 Genioplasty: one central pre-bent 4-hole chin plate with two screws in each segment.27 Each plate is bent onto a smoothed height map of the bone, so it bridges steps rather than sinking into them; screws follow the local plate normal. Ring and bridge widths and screw head shape are illustrative.
