What gum disease is

Gum disease starts with red, swollen gums that bleed when you brush (called gingivitis). You can reverse gingivitis. If it goes on, it can turn into a deeper gum disease (called periodontitis). The gum and the bone that hold the teeth in place slowly wear away. Teeth can then become loose, move, or need to come out.78

There is no cure for this deeper gum disease, but you and your dentist can control it.7 Treatment aims to stop more loss. Lost bone mostly does not grow back. Surgery can partly rebuild some deep, narrow dips in the bone.4 Smoking, and diabetes that is not well controlled, make gum disease more likely and worse.81

Signs include bleeding, red or swollen gums, and gums that shrink back. Others are bad breath, a bad taste, sore teeth when you bite, and loose or moving teeth.8 See a dentist if your gums keep bleeding.

Stages I to IV and grades A to C

Dentists use a system from 2017 to describe gum disease.32 The stage (I to IV) shows how bad it is and how hard it is to treat. Stage I is the mildest. The grade (A to C) shows how fast it is getting worse.1 Your dentist works these out from a gum check, X-rays, your age and your health. For example, smoking and diabetes can mean a faster grade.1

The models show the bone only. You cannot see a grade in a model, so there are no grade models.

For clinicians: 2017 staging and grading tables

How dentists stage periodontitis

The current classification comes from the 2017 World Workshop of the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP).32 The stage (I to IV) describes how severe the disease is and how complex it is to manage. The grade (A to C) describes how fast it is getting worse.1 The EFP treatment guideline for Stages I to III is built on this system.4

The models show bone only, so they show the bone loss row. Attachment loss and pocket depth are measured on the gum with a probe, and the gum is not modelled.

Grades A to C: how fast it is progressing

Two people can have the same stage, but one may have got there over 40 years and the other over 10. The grade describes this rate, and the risk that the disease will keep getting worse or respond less well to treatment.1 A grade cannot be seen in a single model, because it depends on age, history and risk factors, so there are no separate grade models.

Worked examples (bone loss ÷ age)

The ratio uses the bone loss at the worst-affected tooth, as a percentage of root length, divided by age in years.1 Using the bone loss built into these models:

  • The Stage III model (40% bone loss) in a 30-year-old: 40 ÷ 30 = 1.33, more than 1.0, so Grade C.
  • The same Stage III model in a 60-year-old: 40 ÷ 60 = 0.67, so Grade B.
  • The Stage I model (10% bone loss) in a 50-year-old: 10 ÷ 50 = 0.2, less than 0.25, so Grade A.

These examples only show how the ratio works. A real grade also uses direct evidence, the case phenotype and the risk factors above.

For clinicians: how the models were made

All five models start from the same adult lower jaw and 14 teeth (no wisdom teeth) from Z-Anatomy. For each tooth, the CEJ was placed using typical crown and root lengths for lower teeth.6 In the healthy model the bone crest sits 1.5 mm below the CEJ,5 higher between the teeth where the CEJ curves up.6 For each stage, the bone was lowered by a set percentage of root length (Stage I 10%, II 25%, III 40%, IV 60%), chosen to sit inside each stage's bone loss band.1 Stages III and IV add the complexity features listed under each stage.

Simplifications: bone loss is mostly even around the whole jaw (generalised); real periodontitis is often uneven. The gum, root surfaces and tooth movement other than one tipped molar are not modelled. Percentages are measured along the vertical, not the tooth's own long axis.
