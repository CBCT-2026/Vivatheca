    What the model shows

    The model shows one lower baby back tooth (the second baby molar) in a block of gum and bone, as on a teaching model. Under it, the adult tooth that will replace it (the second premolar) is growing in the jaw. Choose a stage to see the tooth healthy, with a large area of decay, with a large filling, trimmed for a crown, and with a stainless steel crown. Tap See-through to look inside: you can see the nerve (pulp, red), the trimmed tooth under the crown, and the growing adult tooth.

    What is a stainless steel crown?

    A stainless steel crown is a ready-made metal cap. It covers the whole of a baby back tooth, down to just under the gum. Dentists use it when a baby molar has decay on several surfaces, after treatment of the nerve inside the tooth (a pulpotomy or pulpectomy), when a tooth is broken or did not form properly, and for children at high risk of new decay.14

    Usually the tooth is numbed, the decay is removed, and the top and the sides that touch the next teeth are trimmed a little so the crown fits. The crown is then pressed on and cemented.2 The baby tooth, with its crown, falls out in the normal way when the adult tooth comes through.

    Why a crown instead of a large filling?

    A filling replaces only the part of the tooth that was lost. When decay is large and runs between the teeth, the walls of tooth left around a filling are thin. A crown covers every surface, so it holds the tooth together and leaves no edges between filling and tooth where new decay can start.14

    What studies found when crowns were compared with fillings in baby molars
    SourceFinding
    Cochrane systematic review, 20152Crowns probably lower the risk of a major failure (such as an abscess or the tooth needing to come out) in the long term, compared with fillings: risk ratio 0.18 (3 trials, 346 teeth, moderate-quality evidence). Pain in the long term was also less likely with crowns: risk ratio 0.15 (2 trials, 312 teeth).
    Systematic review, 20005Across 10 studies, 1.9 to 30.3 % of steel crowns failed, compared with 11.6 to 88.7 % of amalgam (silver) fillings.
    Split-mouth study, 2021 (988 baby molars)6After 2 years, 92.8 % of crowns were still working, compared with 80.0 % of fillings that covered several surfaces. The difference was largest in children treated at 4 years or younger.
    Randomised trial, Hall Technique, 5 years3Major failures: 3 % of crowns and 16.5 % of fillings. Minor failures (such as a lost filling or new decay): 5 % of crowns and 42 % of fillings.

    The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry guideline says steel crowns are indicated for children at high risk of decay with large or multi-surface decay in baby molars, especially when they need treatment under sedation or general anaesthesia.1 It also notes that most of the evidence for crowns comes from non-randomised and retrospective studies, and that a small filling can still be the right choice for a small hole.1

    The Hall Technique

    In the Hall Technique, the dentist cements a steel crown over the baby molar without numbing, without removing the decay and without trimming the tooth. The crown seals the decay in, which controls it instead of removing it.13 The model's Trimmed stage shows the usual (conventional) method; with the Hall Technique, the crown would sit over the untrimmed tooth.

    Prototype, version 0.1. The baby molar is made by resizing an adult first molar to typical lower second baby molar sizes (crown 9.9 mm front to back, 8.7 mm cheek to tongue, crown 5.5 mm tall, roots 9.8 mm),7 and spreading the roots apart, as baby molar roots are. Gum and bone are simple blocks, as on a teaching model, and the neighbouring teeth are left out. The size and place of the decay, the amount of trimming and the crown thickness are illustrations, not measurements; the crown wall is shown thicker than a real crown so it can be 3D printed.
