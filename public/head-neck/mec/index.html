Cancer of a small spit gland in the roof of the mouth (mucoepidermoid carcinoma)

This 3D model follows one real, published case. A man aged 42 had a slow-growing lump on the right side of the roof of his mouth. It was a slow type of cancer that starts in a small spit gland. The surgeon took out the lump with a cuff of normal lining and kept the bone above it.1

    Before surgery. The cancer (dark red) is a soft lump in the lining of the roof of the mouth, just right of the middle. It is 1 to 1.5 cm across. The bone above it is thin but not broken.

    What is removed. The lump comes out with about 5 mm of normal lining around it and the thin layer that covers the bone. The bone is kept.

    The raw area. The bone is bare where the lining was. No teeth are lost, and there is no hole into the nose.

    Healing. A clear plastic plate clips onto the teeth. It holds a soft dressing paste over the raw area and protects it while new lining grows. By 4 months the roof of the mouth had healed.

    The real case

    A man aged 42 had a painless lump on the roof of his mouth for about a year. His dentist found it at a check-up. A small piece of tissue showed this cancer. Scans showed a lump of 15 × 12 × 5 mm. The bone above it was thin but not broken, and the cancer had not spread.1

    The team chose to take out the lump with a cuff of normal lining and keep the bone. During surgery the layer on the bone looked healthy, so the bone stayed. A plate with dressing paste covered the area.1

    Tests on the removed tissue showed clear edges. No more treatment was needed, only regular checks. He healed with no hole into the nose.1

    How the roof of the mouth heals

    When the bone is kept, the raw area heals by itself under a protective plate, as in this case.1

    Other ways to close a gap in the roof of the mouth: a flap of nearby palate lining can be turned to cover it. A large soft-tissue gap can be closed with a thin skin flap from the forearm (Diagram 2).2 If the cancer has grown into the bone, the bone is removed too. That leaves a hole into the nose or sinus, which is closed with a plate that carries teeth (obturator) or with a flap.3

    For clinicians: how this model was built

    • Case: 42-year-old man; right paramedian hard palate swelling, 10–12 mm clinically, 15 × 12 × 5 mm on MRI with cortical thinning but no breach. Wide local excision with periosteum; periosteum uninvolved intra-operatively, so no bone resection; palatal cover plate with Coe-Pak. Histology: 6 mm MEC, AFIP low grade, Brandwein intermediate grade; peripheral margins over 5 mm, deep margin 0.2 mm with a clear periosteal layer, no bone invasion. MDT: surveillance only.1
    • The authors cite Ord et al.: in low-grade palatal MEC without radiological bone invasion, mucosal excision alone gave no rise in local recurrence over 5 years. Grade, stage and margin status matter more than routine bone removal.1
    • Model: lesion 12 × 15 × 5 mm, centred 7.5 mm right of the midline at the 14/15 level (from Image 1); excision 22 × 25 mm (5 mm cuff), crossing the midline by 3.5 mm; bone and teeth unchanged. Clear cover plate 2 mm thick over the whole palate, with dressing in the defect.
